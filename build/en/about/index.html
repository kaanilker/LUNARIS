About

LUNARIS

Lunar Illumination & Communication Analysis System

LUNARIS is an open source web tool that compares landing sites and dates at the lunar south pole against two conditions: the Sun for power and Earth for communication. It derives each candidate site’s horizon from NASA ephemerides and LOLA terrain data, then calculates when the Sun and Earth stay above that horizon.

What it does

The six views of the site each answer a different question on top of the same data package.

  • Home 01 Summarises current Sun and Earth conditions at the south pole and introduces polar geometry. Live polar indicators and 3D globe
  • Planner 02 Calculates Sun and Earth measures for 130 sites over a chosen date and a seven-day surface window. 7-day window, 130 sites
  • Site detail 03 Shows a site’s 360° terrain horizon, Sun and Earth tracks, and hourly timeline. 360° horizon and sky tracks
  • Compare 04 Puts selected sites side by side over the same time window and measures. Side-by-side metric table
  • Expert analysis 05 Explores sites in depth with filters and measure distributions. C1, C2, C6, D2 distribution chart
  • Missions 06 Shows past and planned south pole missions on the lunar globe. Mission coordinates and logbook

Data sources

Six data products we downloaded for calculation and three tools linked for manual cross-checks.

  • DE440, 1849–2150 NASA SPICE (NAIF) Time-dependent positions of the Sun, Earth, and Moon, and lunar orientation. DE440 ephemeris lunar orientation DSN stations
  • 10–80 m/pixel DEM LRO LOLA (PDS Geosciences) Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter elevation maps; the basis of terrain horizons and slope. South pole DEM permanent shadow mask
  • 60–120 m/pixel Mazarico et al. 2011 (PGDA) Average Sun and Earth visibility maps computed from LOLA; used for comparison in validation. Average Sun visibility average Earth visibility
  • 130 candidate sites Wueller et al. 2026 (Zenodo) 130 landing sites within Artemis candidate regions and an annual illumination model. 130 candidate sites annual illumination PSR volatile estimates
  • 1:5,000,000 scale USGS Unified Geologic Map The unified geologic map of the Moon; the geologic unit of each site. Geologic units region boundaries
  • 30 + 192 test cases JPL Horizons JPL’s independent ephemeris service; used to test the sky calculation. Sun and Earth azimuth elevation

Method

The calculation runs in three steps: sky geometry, local terrain horizon, and hourly visibility windows.

1. Sky position

Sun and Earth vectors from the SPICE DE440 ephemeris are projected onto the site’s local east-north-up (ENU) axes.

2. 360° terrain horizon

720 azimuth rays are traced up to 150 km across the LOLA DEM; lunar curvature is subtracted to obtain H(A).

3. Window and decision

Exposed solar disk fraction and the 2° Earth threshold are counted hourly to produce C1, C2, C6, and D2.

Formulas used

Sky position

F1

Where the Sun and Earth appear from the site, and how high.

ŝ = (r_sky − r_site) / |r_sky − r_site|; e = asin(ŝ·û); A = atan2(ŝ·ê, ŝ·n̂)
Technical detail

SPICE position vectors are reduced by the observer vector at each candidate point. Projection onto local up, east, and north ENU axes gives elevation and azimuth clockwise from north.

Terrain horizon

F2

The angle of the highest obstruction in every direction, including lunar curvature.

H(A) = max_d [ atan((z(A,d) − z₀ − h) / d) − d/(2R) ]
Technical detail

A ray is sampled through the DEM for each azimuth. The highest obstruction along the line of sight defines the horizon angle; lunar curvature lowers the apparent horizon with distance. Profiles use 720 azimuths at 0.5° spacing.

Visible disk fraction

F3

The fraction of the Sun and Earth disks above the horizon.

g = A_visible / (πr²); Earth visible ⇔ e⊕ > max(H(A⊕), 2°)
Technical detail

The Sun and Earth are not treated as point sources. The disk is divided into 16 vertical strips to account for a varying horizon across it; midpoint integration estimates the exposed area fraction. Sunlight uses the packaged threshold, while Earth visibility uses the horizon and a 2° geometric threshold.

Time measures

F4

Longest sunlit and dark periods, and the share of time Earth is visible.

C1 = max consecutive(sunlit)·Δt; C2 = max consecutive(dark)·Δt; D2 = Σ Earth-visible / N
Technical detail

Hourly ephemeris samples yield C1, the longest continuous sunlight; C2, the longest continuous darkness; C6, the long-term sunlight fraction; and D2, the fraction of time Earth is geometrically visible. Planner metrics use a 7-day window from the selected start.

Slope and distance to shadow

F5

Ground slope and straight-line distance to permanently shadowed regions.

∂z/∂x, ∂z/∂y ≈ 3×3 Horn; d_PSR = EDT(mask_PSR)
Technical detail

The pipeline derives slope on the DEM with the 3×3 Horn finite-difference operator. Distance to permanent shadow is computed with a Euclidean distance transform. C4s and C5 are derived from these layers (below).

Gates and TOPSIS

F6

Orders sites with classic TOPSIS over seven measures at equal weight; gates do not exclude sites.

vᵢⱼ = wⱼ·xᵢⱼ / √Σᵢxᵢⱼ²; Cᵢ* = Sᵢ⁻ / (Sᵢ⁺ + Sᵢ⁻)
Technical detail

The decision engine implements PASS/FAIL/MARGINAL/UNKNOWN gates, benefit/cost directions, and classical vector-normalized TOPSIS. The Planner and Expert Analysis order the sites with equal-weight TOPSIS over seven measures (C1, C2, C4s, C5, C6, C7, D₂). Missing values are never replaced with zero; a site with an unknown measure is not ranked and the reason is shown. Equal weights and the use of the C4s and C5 proxy measures are a user decision (2026-11-15) and may change later.

C4s and C5 proxy measures

C4s is the probability that the largest slope in the 100 m landing area exceeds the 10° limit: 1 − Φ((10° − θmax) / σ), σ = 2.0°. The slope limit is from NASA HLS Update 2024 p.19; σ is the midpoint of the 1.5–2.5° range Barker et al. 2021 give for a 5 m DEM. Carrying that value over to the 10 m DEM has no source.

C5 is the walking time from the site to the nearest LOLA permanent shadow cell. The route is the shortest path over cells at or below 20° on the LOLA 10 m DEM (10 km search radius) at a fixed 2 km/h. The speed is the Artemis III EVA planning value of Scoville 2021 (NTRS 20210026296), the slope limit is xEVA SRD Annex J-02 and NASA HLS Update 2024 p.21. Distance is horizontal; speed does not vary with slope and the shadow cell’s temperature is not checked.

Architecture detail

Scientific data pipeline

The Python pipeline uses spiceypy for SPICE kernels; NumPy and SciPy for numerical work; Rasterio/GDAL for LOLA rasters; and pyproj for polar stereographic transforms. Horizon and ephemeris computations run ahead of time; raw DEMs are not sent to the browser.

Web application

Svelte 5 and SvelteKit provide pages and components; TypeScript runs the browser calculation engine; Vite handles development and builds; adapter-static produces cPanel-ready files. SVG, Canvas 2D, and GSAP power charts, the lunar globe, and interface motion.

Versioned data package

Site metadata is JSON; horizon profiles use int16 values in 0.01° units; hourly sky vectors use float32 binary files in kilometres. The manifest records sources, parameter version, time axis, and DEM resolution. The current package covers 130 sites and 2026–2033.

Reference calculation and tests

The Python pipeline is the reference implementation for geometry; the TypeScript engine is compared against its golden files. Pytest checks the pipeline, Vitest checks geometry and UI utilities, and Playwright checks browser flows. A PHP endpoint proxies openai/gpt-oss-20b requests to Groq; the API key stays server-side.

Globe and map projection

Points on the lunar globe and coordinates of the polar map. The home page point globe distributes surface points evenly using the Fibonacci angle and uses orthographic projection. The polar map uses a south polar stereographic projection; the data pipeline prepares map boundaries and coordinate transforms.

α = π(3−√5); ρ = 2R tan(π/4 + φ/2); x = ρ sinλ, y = ρ cosλ
